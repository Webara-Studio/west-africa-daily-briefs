# 📦 Import & Export Trade Trends — Sunday, 1 November 2026 — EXPORT Week

## Headline Trends
Week 44 is an **EXPORT week** under the brief’s ISO-week rotation. Ghana’s latest published annual merchandise-trade snapshot is striking: the Ghana Statistical Service reported a GH¢148.3bn surplus for 2025, with gold the dominant export driver. A separate GoldBod-reported figure puts artisanal and small-scale gold exports at 85.4 tonnes, valued at US$11.6bn through the reported 2026 period; the underlying statement is a year-to-date report, not a final audited full-year total.

The wider signal is less comfortable: export receipts are rising, but concentration in gold and unprocessed commodities leaves the region exposed to price swings, policy shifts and missed processing margins. Recent reporting also points to cargo delays and an intensifying contest among Tema, Abidjan and Lomé for regional container business.

## Sentiment Snapshot
**Mixed, with a bullish near-term revenue picture and a cautious structural outlook.** Strong gold receipts and a sizeable Ghana trade surplus support FX supply and fiscal revenues. Yet cocoa’s production and financing pressures, policy uncertainty around gold and raw-mineral exports, and port-side delays make it risky to treat headline export values as evidence of broad-based competitiveness. These values are nominal and do not by themselves establish higher physical volumes or domestic value capture.

## Deep Dive
### 1. Top West African Exports — Current Volumes & Trends

**Gold — Ghana.** The most consequential current data point is the reported 85.4 tonnes of small-scale gold exported for US$11.6bn in 2026 through the period covered by GoldBod’s October report. Treat both figures as a reported year-to-date tally pending reconciliation against customs and final annual accounts. The value underscores the export channel’s importance, but also the risks: concentration, traceability, illicit leakage and the foreign-exchange consequences of policy changes. A September report said the Bank of Ghana warned that an export halt could pressure reserves and the current account. GoldBod-linked reporting also described domestic refinery partnerships lifting local processing to 94.6% in October; this is a reported share, not proof of equivalent national refining capacity or net domestic value added.

**Cocoa — Ghana and Côte d’Ivoire.** Cocoa remains the region’s signature agricultural export, but the business is constrained by supply and finance rather than by a lack of global recognition. In October, reporting described Ghana’s cocoa regulator raising US$288m to support farmer purchasing and liquidity. That is financing context, not export tonnage. Côte d’Ivoire reportedly sold 400,000 tonnes of 2025/26 cocoa export contracts in March; contracted volume must not be confused with shipped or customs-cleared exports. Ghana’s ongoing output weakness and the need for working capital make reliable procurement, quality control and delivery performance central commercial issues.

**Cashew and shea.** Both remain commercially relevant regional crops, especially across Côte d’Ivoire, Ghana, Benin, Burkina Faso and Mali, but this run did not establish a fresh, independently verified 2026 regional export-volume series. Raw nuts and kernels, shea kernels and butter are distinct products with different prices and standards; do not use production estimates as export totals. The practical opportunity is quality-assured aggregation, grading, moisture control and buyer-backed first-stage processing. Confirm seasonal availability and buyer specifications with producer groups and exporters before committing inventory.

**Bauxite.** Guinea is the region’s major bauxite exporter. Available reported data include 99.8 million tonnes in 2024 (up 36% year on year) and a 23% year-on-year rise in third-quarter 2025 exports despite weather and regulatory pressure. These are historical reported figures, not a current 2026 shipment tally. In March 2026, Reuters reported that Guinea was considering export quotas or curbs; implementation and realised shipment effects require confirmation. Aluminium prices are not a bauxite price proxy for realised mine-gate or FOB economics.

**Crude oil.** Nigeria remains a major regional crude exporter, but the balance between exports and domestic refining is changing. NUPRC-reported coverage says domestic refineries received 127 million barrels over the first nine months of 2026. That number is domestic supply, not total production or exports; it signals a larger domestic pull on crude availability and makes export-volume comparisons dependent on field output, losses and refinery liftings. Check NUPRC monthly data and cargo nominations before pricing a physical trade.

**Rubber.** Ghanaian raw-rubber exports are a policy and value-retention flashpoint. 2026 reporting alleged under-declaration and estimated as much as US$70m in foregone value; the regulator disputed some allegations, so the figure should be treated as a contested report, not an audited loss. A temporary raw-rubber export restriction was also reported in July. Exporters and processors should verify current rules, permits and customs valuation before buying or shipping; a policy pause can rapidly strand inventory or redirect supply.

**Timber.** The region’s timber trade is increasingly shaped by legality, traceability and deforestation-linked market access, particularly for EU-bound supply. No comparable current export-volume figure was verified for this brief. Treat timber opportunities as compliance-led: document species, origin, permits and chain of custody, and confirm destination-specific due-diligence requirements before signing contracts.

### 2. New Export Opportunities

1. **Export enablement for smaller suppliers:** provide pooled quality testing, grading, traceability, consolidated documentation and shipment tracking for cocoa, cashew, shea, rubber and timber suppliers. Revenue can come from service fees or contracted exporter arrangements, with less capital risk than owning commodity stock.
2. **Modular processing with contracted buyers:** explore cocoa semi-finished products, cashew kernels and shea butter only where feedstock, energy, water, lab testing, working capital and offtake are secured. Start with toll-processing or a small production run; scale only after a buyer accepts samples and repeat orders.
3. **Regional logistics and transit services:** Takoradi’s reported French-language transit-trade strategy points to opportunity in documentation, forwarding and inland distribution serving Francophone Sahel markets. The offer must solve measurable border, road, insurance and cargo-visibility problems, not simply promise a faster port.
4. **Mineral upgrading:** Ghana’s reported policy ambition to end unprocessed mineral exports by 2030 creates demand for feasibility work, local testing, energy solutions and buyer qualification. It is a policy direction, not a guaranteed ban timetable or an investable project by itself.

### 3. Export Infrastructure

**Port activity and reliability.** The available current reporting is directional rather than a harmonised port-throughput dataset. In October, the Loadstar reported that West African congestion is not confined to terminals: landside access, trucking and logistics coordination are part of the constraint. Ghanaian trade reporting warned that delays risk diverting import and transit business towards Abidjan. Takoradi is positioning itself for French-speaking transit trade, while Tema, Abidjan and Lomé compete for container flows. For exporters, the relevant metric is door-to-vessel time by corridor, not port rankings alone.

**Exporter economics.** Delays create demurrage, storage and working-capital costs, while uncertain inspection and trucking schedules can jeopardise quality-sensitive cargo. Trade.gov’s Ghana customs guide describes lengthy clearance procedures, congested port roads and 60–80% of imports subject to inspection; those figures concern imports and should not be misrepresented as an export inspection rate. It also states that the ICUMS fee is 0.75% of FOB value, with an additional 1% processing fee applicable to some imports. Obtain product- and transaction-specific confirmation before budgeting.

No reliable, current corridor-specific carrier quote was found for Tema, Takoradi, Abidjan or Lagos. Shipping cost is route-, equipment-, season- and contract-dependent; request live freight and inland-haulage quotations rather than relying on a generic regional figure. Nigeria’s Lekki port and Lagos terminals, and the Tema–Abidjan–Lomé competition, make port choice and final inland delivery a commercial optimisation problem, not a branding decision.

### 4. Value-Add Trends and Imports in Brief

There is a visible policy and commercial push towards more domestic mineral and agricultural processing, and GoldBod reporting highlights refinery partnerships. However, local processing is not automatically profitable: plants need dependable feedstock, energy, tested product quality, working capital, compliant licensing and contracted buyers. Ghana’s raw-rubber controversy illustrates the danger of restricting exports before domestic processors can absorb supply at a competitive price.

On the **import side**, Ghana, Nigeria and Côte d’Ivoire continue to rely on imports for products including refined petroleum and fuels, rice and wheat, machinery, electronics and medicines, though this brief did not establish fresh comparable customs tonnages for the three countries. The Ghana customs guide confirms practical clearance friction and ICUMS-related charges. Import substitution is most credible in carefully selected food processing, packaging and basic industrial inputs where domestic feedstock and reliable demand exist; pharmaceuticals and machinery require stronger standards, capital and regulatory controls. Do not infer a particular new tariff or ban from general reporting: validate current HS-code treatment in the relevant customs tariff and legal notice.

**Trade arrangements.** AfCFTA and ECOWAS preferences can widen regional markets, but eligibility, rules of origin, product standards and customs execution determine whether the preference has real value. Verify the origin rule and required certificate for each product and route; a tariff preference is not a substitute for clearance planning.

## Commercial Opportunity
**Best near-term angle: a lean export-readiness and shipment-coordination service for Ghanaian cashew, shea and cocoa SMEs using Tema or Takoradi, with an initial pilot focused on one product and one destination buyer.** Bundle verified supplier onboarding, lot-level quality/moisture checks, traceability records, export documentation, inspection scheduling, forwarder coordination and milestone-based payment visibility. Avoid financing commodity inventory in the first phase.

**Pilot test:** recruit 5–10 suppliers, secure one buyer’s written specifications and sample acceptance, and manage 3–5 consolidated shipments. Track rejection rate, days from purchase to vessel departure, avoidable storage/demurrage per tonne, buyer repeat intent and gross margin per shipment. Compare an actual Tema/Takoradi quotation and transit schedule with an Abidjan or Lomé alternative before positioning the service around a specific corridor. The model wins if it reduces failed lots and shipment variance enough to earn recurring fees—not merely because the region has export potential.

## Watch List
- **GoldBod policy and exports:** distinguish a pause, allocation change or rule proposal from an enacted restriction; changes affect FX inflows, supply chains and counterparties.
- **Ghana mineral-processing policy through 2030:** track the legal instrument, transition dates, exemptions and demonstrated local processing/offtake capacity.
- **Cocoa financing and crop deliveries:** monitor farmer payments, licensed buying, arrivals and the conversion of contracted volume into shipped exports.
- **Port access and service reliability:** collect current carrier schedules, truck turnaround, dwell time, demurrage and inland transit data on each target route.
- **Raw-rubber export controls and valuation:** confirm operative rules, exemptions and the regulator’s response to under-invoicing allegations before sourcing.
