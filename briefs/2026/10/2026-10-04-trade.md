# 📦 Export Trade Trends — Sunday, 4 October 2026 — EXPORT Week

## Headline Trends

The ISO week number is **40**, an even week: this brief therefore takes exports as its principal focus. The clearest broad signal is a strong nominal expansion in reported 2025 merchandise exports for Ghana and Côte d’Ivoire, with Nigeria also higher but growing more slowly. The data are in current US dollars, not physical volumes, so higher values can reflect prices, exchange-rate conversion, reporting changes and quantities; they should not be read as proof of equivalent real-volume growth.

The export proposition remains commodity-led. Ghana’s gold and cocoa, Nigeria’s crude oil and gas, and Côte d’Ivoire’s cocoa, cashew and other agricultural exports dominate attention. The strategic question is increasingly how to capture more of the processing, logistics and compliance margin locally, rather than merely ship raw output.

## Sentiment Snapshot

**Mixed, with a constructive bias.** Higher nominal trade values support an optimistic revenue backdrop, but exporters face concentration risk, crop and production variability, FX uncertainty, working-capital pressure and demanding traceability requirements. Headline prices alone do not guarantee better farm-gate returns or reliable shipment margins. Investors and operators should underwrite contracts on conservative realised prices and confirmed logistics costs.

Imports remain relevant to the picture: World Bank-reported 2025 merchandise imports were approximately US$17.5bn for Ghana, US$41.5bn for Nigeria and US$19.3bn for Côte d’Ivoire. These totals show the scale of the regional import market and the potential for local supply substitution, but are not product-level import shares.

## Deep Dive

### Export performance and product signals

The World Bank’s latest reported annual merchandise totals available in the queried series show the following (current US dollars; rounded):

| Country | Exports 2024 | Exports 2025 | Imports 2025 | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Ghana | US$19.2bn | US$31.1bn | US$17.5bn | Very large nominal increase; reconcile against commodity prices, physical output and revisions before projecting cash flows. |
| Nigeria | US$53.3bn | US$55.5bn | US$41.5bn | Modest nominal export expansion; energy remains central, leaving exposure to production outages and global oil prices. |
| Côte d’Ivoire | US$20.5bn | US$28.5bn | US$19.3bn | Strong nominal increase; cocoa and agricultural trade are important, but values do not isolate volumes or product mix. |

Source: World Bank WITS merchandise-export/import indicators. The figures are annual, not live shipment statistics; 2025 values may be subject to revision. They do not identify the contribution of individual products.

- **Cocoa:** Ghana and Côte d’Ivoire remain the region’s pivotal origins. Tight supply, weather and disease risks, quality, financing and traceability shape the economics. Exporters should not infer current crop volumes from annual trade values. Cocoa liquor, butter, powder and chocolate ingredients can retain more value locally, but require consistent quality, energy, packaging, food-safety systems and secured offtake.
- **Gold:** Ghana’s gold exports are a major source of foreign exchange. The commercial opportunity extends beyond production to responsible sourcing, assaying, aggregation and compliant refining. Gold is high value-to-weight, but licensing, origin verification, security and counterparty controls are non-negotiable. GoldBod is the key national source to monitor for Ghana’s gold-sector rules and announcements.
- **Cashew:** Côte d’Ivoire is a major global origin; Ghana also has a base from which to build aggregation and processing. Kernel processing can create local jobs and value, but margins depend on recovery rates, raw nut quality, reliable power, labour productivity and buyer specifications. Do not build capacity without feedstock and offtake contracts.
- **Shea:** Northern Ghana and neighbouring Sahel markets have a strong supply base. Shea butter and derivatives can serve food, cosmetics and personal-care buyers. The bottlenecks are seasonal aggregation, consistent grading, contamination control, traceability and access to working capital; small-batch premium positioning may be more viable than undifferentiated bulk exports.
- **Bauxite:** Ghana has bauxite resources, but mining and export plans must be assessed alongside transport, environmental and land impacts, and the long-term economics of domestic refining. Bauxite is not a quick, low-capital export play; infrastructure and permitting determine viability.
- **Crude oil:** Nigeria and Ghana are regional producers, with export performance sensitive to production reliability, maintenance, theft/security and price. Local refining and gas utilisation alter the balance between crude exports and imported refined products; capacity and actual output matter more than announcements.
- **Rubber:** Côte d’Ivoire is the leading regional producer. Natural rubber exports depend on global demand, plantation productivity, collection networks and shipment economics. Local conversion into technically specified rubber products is an opportunity, though it requires reliable quality control and industrial buyers.
- **Timber:** Ghana and Côte d’Ivoire have forestry and wood-product export potential, but legality, sustainable harvest, chain-of-custody and destination-market rules govern access. The stronger value proposition is verified, processed wood products rather than untraceable raw timber.

Product-specific current volumes for all eight commodities are not published in the World Bank country totals used here. Avoid quoting precise tonnages without customs, ministry or commodity-board data for a defined period and grade.

### New export opportunities and value addition

1. **Cocoa ingredients and branded regional products:** Start with buyer-led cocoa liquor, butter, powder or couverture supply rather than a speculative consumer brand. Build on verified origin, consistent specifications and contracted demand.
2. **Cashew kernels and by-products:** Contract farmers/aggregators and a processor, then target buyers seeking traceable West African kernels. Shell-liquid recovery may offer a further industrial opportunity where environmental and safety controls are viable.
3. **Shea ingredients:** Standardised, tested shea butter and cosmetic-grade inputs can earn a premium over mixed, ungraded nuts, especially with documented origin and dependable batch consistency.
4. **Regional processed food and ingredients:** AfCFTA and ECOWAS frameworks provide a basis for regional trade, but tariff treatment is product- and origin-specific. Confirm rules of origin, standards, labelling and border procedures before pricing a route.
5. **Responsible minerals and verified forestry products:** Documented chain-of-custody and credible environmental and social safeguards increasingly form part of market access, not optional branding.

Value addition is advancing unevenly. Ghana’s cocoa processing and Côte d’Ivoire’s cashew/cocoa capacity show movement, but scale is constrained by finance, power reliability, technology, raw-material seasonality and competition for feedstock. Processing is not automatically more profitable: compare the netback after conversion loss, energy, compliance, packaging, financing and sales costs against raw-export alternatives.

### Export infrastructure and logistics

Tema is Ghana’s principal container gateway, while Takoradi is important for bulk, energy and industrial cargo. Abidjan is a major regional hub; Lagos-area ports handle Nigeria’s large import-export economy. Port and terminal performance vary by cargo type and time. Public port statistics and operator updates should be checked for the actual period before making claims about current throughput or congestion.

For an exporter, the binding logistics variables are often inland haulage, container availability, empty returns, customs documentation, inspection, port dwell time, and reliable cut-off and sailing schedules—not just the ocean freight quote. Shipping prices are route-, equipment-, season- and contract-specific; no single current rate is defensible without a live quote. Obtain comparable door-to-port and port-to-port offers, including surcharges, demurrage/free time, insurance and transit-time assumptions. For perishable products, test reefer access, cold-chain hand-offs and backup power before committing inventory.

### Brief import context

Ghana, Nigeria and Côte d’Ivoire all recorded substantial merchandise imports in the latest World Bank annual series. Food staples, refined fuels, machinery, electronics and pharmaceuticals are recurring import categories across the region, but this brief does not assert country-specific current volumes for rice, wheat or any other category without verified customs data. For potential import substitution, prioritize products with dependable local inputs and buyers—such as selected processed foods, packaging, basic construction inputs and agro-processing—rather than assuming every imported product can be made competitively at home.

## Commercial Opportunity

**Best near-term angle: buyer-backed aggregation and primary processing for cashew or shea, with cocoa ingredients as a higher-capital alternative.** The relatively capital-light route is to secure buyer specifications and indicative offtake first, map compliant producers and aggregators, run representative quality tests, and finance only the volume that can be delivered to grade. Earn margin through cleaning, sorting, grading, traceability and dependable consolidation before investing in a large plant.

A practical sequence:

1. Pick one product, origin corridor and target buyer segment; do not launch three supply chains at once.
2. Obtain written product specifications, sample approval and a price formula from buyers.
3. Verify licences, export documentation, standards, origin evidence and applicable rules of origin with GEPA, customs and relevant regulators.
4. Run a small paid pilot shipment; calculate true netback after farm-gate price, shrinkage, processing, bags, inland transport, inspection, finance, port and freight charges.
5. Scale only when repeat orders, working capital and quality control are proven.

The arbitrage is not simply buying cheap locally and selling dear abroad. It is reducing buyer friction and rejected loads through reliable quality, aggregation and traceability—while preserving enough margin for producers and the operator.

## Watch List

- Ghana Cocoa Board crop, producer-price and sector-policy announcements; harvest and quality outcomes in Ghana and Côte d’Ivoire.
- GoldBod licensing, sourcing and export-policy updates; responsible-sourcing requirements in destination markets.
- Oil production and export reliability in Nigeria and Ghana, alongside refinery output and fuel-import needs.
- Current exchange rates and settlement availability in GHS, NGN and XOF; avoid using a benchmark rate as an executable trade rate.
- Live carrier quotes, container availability, port dwell times and demurrage exposure at Tema, Takoradi, Abidjan and Lagos-area terminals.
- AfCFTA/ECOWAS tariff schedules, rules of origin, standards and any new export restrictions or domestic-supply measures.
