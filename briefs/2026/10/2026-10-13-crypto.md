### West African regulation and CBDCs

- **Ghana:** Bank of Ghana (BoG) and SEC material should be treated as the controlling sources for legal status. Recent coverage says the SEC admitted 11 firms to a crypto sandbox in September; separate earlier reporting describes a five-agency committee coordinating implementation of the virtual-asset framework. A sandbox cohort is not a general VASP licence, and firms must stay within approved activities and any BoG/SEC conditions. Check the live SEC notice and subsequent BoG circulars before onboarding or custody arrangements.
- **Nigeria:** August coverage reported a CBN regulatory sandbox for virtual-asset and fintech firms; earlier reporting described a pilot and AML supervisory work. These reports indicate experimentation and oversight, not permission for every exchange to operate or a reversal of all banking/compliance constraints. SEC registration, CBN sandbox participation and access to banking rails are separate questions. Verify each entity and permitted activity against current regulator notices/registers; directory entries can lag.
- **e-Cedi:** May reports say the project remains active and that BoG is considering cross-border use. No new public launch, nationwide rollout or verified pilot performance result was corroborated in this research. A continuing project or policy ambition should not be marketed as a live CBDC remittance channel.
- **Kenya:** July–September coverage describes 2026 VASP rules and a developing licensing framework. Kenya is a regional comparator, not West Africa. Confirm commencement, regulator allocation, application windows and capital conditions in the enacted instrument before using the rules as an operational precedent.
- **South Africa:** October reporting described Kredete acquiring a CASP licence to expand its footprint. This is a company-specific reported development, not evidence that the South African market has newly opened generally. Confirm the licence holder, scope and FSCA register entry. Separate CASP permissions from banking, custody and cross-border payments permissions.

### Exchange activity

The clearest reported exchange-sector signal was Yellow Card’s US$40 million Series C announcement in August, described as funding African expansion and stablecoin-payment infrastructure. It is a financing announcement, not a measure of exchange trading volume or proof of new Ghanaian/Nigerian product availability. Late-July coverage called Quidax Nigeria’s first SEC-licensed exchange and reported expansion of stablecoin infrastructure to more than 21 countries; confirm scope and legal entity directly with SEC Nigeria and Quidax before relying on either claim. No independently verified current volume change or material new Luno/Busha feature was established in the available reporting.

### Stablecoins, remittances and Web3

Stablecoin-focused products and payment partnerships remain prominent in industry coverage, but this scan did not find audited, corridor-specific UK→Ghana or US→Nigeria stablecoin remittance volumes, all-in customer costs or payout success rates. Generic claims about on-chain transfer value are not equivalent to household remittances. The operational comparison must include FX spread, funding and cash-out fees, compliance costs, payout time, failure rates and prefunding—not merely blockchain fees.

No fresh, well-corroborated West African DeFi, NFT or token-launch milestone emerged from the targeted reporting. This is a research-window finding, not proof that no projects exist. Avoid treating older ecosystem-development headlines as current adoption evidence.

## Commercial Opportunity

The strongest near-term opportunity is **compliant stablecoin settlement-to-MoMo infrastructure**, initially as a tightly controlled B2B pilot rather than a consumer token-trading proposition. Partner with appropriately authorised payment/virtual-asset firms and regulated banks or payment providers; begin with one corridor and one use case (for example, SME supplier payments or diaspora-funded business payouts). Build explicit controls for KYC/AML, wallet screening, conversion and FX quotes, custody boundaries, transaction limits, reconciliation and exception handling.

Measure total cost against established remittance/payment alternatives, payout completion rate, settlement time, liquidity utilisation and customer complaints. Do not claim savings until the pilot measures the complete origin-to-beneficiary journey. The principal risk is regulatory mismatch: a sandbox, foreign licence or technical integration may not confer permission for local custody, exchange, remittance or MoMo payout.

## Watch List

- Ghana SEC/BoG: any updated participant list, activity limits, application steps or full authorisations following sandbox testing.
- Nigeria: primary CBN notice and published sandbox conditions; verify SEC registers and bank-rail rules separately.
- e-Cedi: evidence of an actual cross-border trial, user volumes, settlement design or public deployment timetable.
- Yellow Card, Quidax, Busha and Luno: direct product availability, locally applicable permissions and independently reported operating metrics.
- Stablecoin remittances: matched UK→Ghana and US→Nigeria quotes, including FX, payout fees, speed and failure rates—not headline on-chain totals.
