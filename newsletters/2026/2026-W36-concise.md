# West Africa Weekly — 2026 W36

West Africa’s commercial story this week was a shift from headline ambition to operating infrastructure. Regulation is tightening around digital assets, power and primary markets are opening selectively, while commodity value is moving towards processing, traceability and reliable logistics. The strongest opportunities sit with businesses that can make regional commerce more compliant, financeable and dependable.

## 1. Ghana and Nigeria Move Crypto Towards Compliance-Grade Settlement

Ghana’s SEC crypto sandbox was reported to have expanded to 20 participants, signalling controlled experimentation rather than an open licence to serve the public. Nigeria is moving in the opposite direction: reported proposals include a N30 million registration fee, capital requirements reaching up to N2 billion for some firms, and 80 per cent offline custody of customer assets. Separately, Yellow Card and Tranzmit were reported to be developing a USA–Nigeria stablecoin transfer corridor.

**Why it matters:** The investable opportunity is shifting away from speculative tokens and towards the plumbing of cross-border payments. Banks, remittance firms, exchanges and marketplaces will need wallet-to-fiat reconciliation, transaction monitoring, sanctions screening, treasury reporting and reliable payout-status controls. A corridor-first provider can sell this infrastructure through licensed partners without taking custody or betting its balance sheet on crypto prices — although “software only” does not remove licensing, banking, liquidity or compliance risk.

## 2. Distributed Power and Storage Look More Investable Than Grand Capacity Targets

Nigeria is associated with a reported ambition to reach 62GW of power capacity by 2030, with renewables representing 77 per cent, but that remains a target until backed by an integrated plan, procurement calendar, grid pathway and financing. More concrete signals are emerging in distributed power: reported US$4 million investment in solar mini-grids across 19 communities, battery-led electrification activity across Burkina Faso, Mali, Nigeria and Senegal, and Ghana’s reported procurement of solar battery storage. Ghana also has a proposed GHS1 billion renewable-energy green bond, though issuance terms and approvals are not yet confirmed.

**Why it matters:** The near-term market is not simply panel sales. It is solar-plus-storage for agro-processors, cold stores, hospitals, universities, telecoms and water operators, combined with metering, remote monitoring, billing, replacement planning and local O&M. Developers should underwrite productive demand and payment security before equipment, while financiers can create value by turning verified meter data and contracted cash flows into investable local-currency portfolios. The commercial winners will sell uptime and lifecycle service, not just installed capacity.

## 3. Cocoa Volatility Strengthens the Case for Processing-Linked Agribusiness

Cocoa was volatile but elevated: the latest accessible close in the agribusiness brief was approximately US$6,509 per tonne on 1 September, around 5.75 per cent above the comparable late-August close, despite a 2.12 per cent day-on-day pullback. ICCO’s latest bulletin estimated a 2024/25 global surplus of 37,000 tonnes, while withholding current 2025/26 production and grindings data; Ghana is prioritising export throughput and farmer input support, and Côte d’Ivoire and Nigeria continue to push domestic processing. Weather risk is uneven across the region, with Gulf of Guinea flooding damaging logistics while heat and rainfall deficits affect parts of northern Nigeria and the Sahel; no defensible public daily benchmark was available for cashew, shea or rubber.

**Why it matters:** Raw commodity exposure is a poor substitute for an operating model. The sharper opportunity is contract-led aggregation and semi-processing — cashew, cocoa ingredients or cashew-apple by-products — supported by solar-assisted drying, packhouses, cold storage and decentralised warehousing. Start with one product, one named buyer and a written specification; earn from throughput and quality rather than guessing the next futures move. Climate resilience and traceability are becoming margin protection, not optional ESG decoration.

## 4. African Equities Hold Up, but the Real Catalyst Is the Primary-Market Pipeline

On the latest verified closes, BRVM rose 1.57 per cent and Kenya’s NSE All Share Index added 0.29 per cent on 3 September, while Ghana’s GSE Composite eased 0.85 per cent after a remarkable 68.29 per cent year-to-date rise. Nigeria’s NGX All-Share Index gained 0.15 per cent, and reporting around FTSE Russell adding 31 Nigerian stocks is improving institutional visibility. The most watched issuance story is Dangote Refinery: an IPO application was reported as filed, but the timetable, size, pricing and non-resident eligibility still require primary-document confirmation.

**Why it matters:** Strong index performance is useful, but durable capital-market development depends on credible issuance, liquidity and access. A diaspora-ready primary-market service could combine verified offer documents, non-resident KYC, transparent FX pricing, local custody, allocation updates and post-listing visibility through licensed brokers and banks. Issuers also need investor-relations and readiness support. The opportunity is not another generic trading interface; it is reducing the trust and administrative friction around high-profile African offers.

## 5. Gold Formalisation and Refining Are Rewriting Who Captures Export Value

West African exports are increasingly being shaped by processing, documentation and clearance rather than commodity prices alone. Ghana’s GoldBod formalisation drive was associated with reported exports of about 170 tonnes worth roughly US$17 billion by August, figures that require reconciliation with official customs and GoldBod data; Nigeria’s Dangote refinery is also associated with falling fuel imports and rising potential for regional product exports. Côte d’Ivoire’s cashew-processing expansion and cocoa traceability requirements reinforce the same direction of travel.

**Why it matters:** Formalisation may compress margins for undocumented traders while expanding demand for licensed aggregation, assay, secure logistics, vaulting, refinery services and responsible-sourcing evidence. The most defensible business is an export-enablement layer launched on one corridor and one product — for example, Ghanaian gold or processed cashew — connecting qualified suppliers to an overseas buyer with batch records, laboratory evidence, customs documentation and finance-ready reporting. Measure rejected consignments, clearance time, shrinkage, payment days and realised FOB price before taking principal commodity exposure.
