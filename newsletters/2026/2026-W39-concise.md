# West Africa Weekly — 2026, Week 39

This week’s strongest signals point to markets moving from ambition towards execution: Ghana’s new statutory perimeter for virtual-asset firms, practical solar-plus-storage deployment in Nigeria, and shifting commodity and trade economics. Capital-market headlines are energetic, but several high-profile offerings remain plans rather than investable transactions. Across the region, the commercial edge lies in verifying what is operational, financeable and permitted—not simply what has been announced.

## 1. Ghana’s VASP law turns crypto compliance into a market-entry question

Ghana’s virtual-asset framework has moved beyond a registration exercise: the Bank of Ghana identifies the Virtual Asset Service Providers Act, 2025 (Act 1154) as the basis for registration, licensing and supervision, with responsibilities involving the BoG and SEC depending on the activity. Licensing categories, fees, transitional treatment and implementation deadlines still require confirmation from the regulators. Meanwhile, stablecoins remain the more practical crypto use case for cross-border settlement; BTC and ETH’s sharp global rebound this week is not evidence of Africa-led flows.

**Why it matters:** A clearer statutory perimeter can make Ghana more investable for compliant exchanges, custody providers and payments infrastructure, while raising the cost of casual market entry. The immediate opportunity is not to launch another speculative token product; it is to help businesses meet transaction-monitoring, audit, custody and reporting requirements, or to build B2B settlement tools with regulated fiat on- and off-ramps. Any operator should map its exact activity to the applicable licence before committing capital or marketing services: registration alone should not be treated as authorisation.

## 2. Cocoa’s short rally does not erase the surplus—and Ghana is attacking logistics

The latest available cocoa benchmark close, on 22 September, was US$5,417.68 per tonne: up 1.25% on the day but down 8.87% over a month and 22.52% year on year. The International Cocoa Organization’s August bulletin estimated a 37,000-tonne global surplus for 2025/26. In Ghana, the Cocoa Marketing Company has moved to 24-hour operations, alongside climate-smart farmer training and engagement on free input distribution; a reported potential 16% fall in 2026/27 output remains a risk estimate, not a confirmed harvest result.

**Why it matters:** Exporters and processors should not mistake a single-session bounce for a structural shortage or build an unhedged inventory position around it. Ghana’s operational changes may be more valuable: shorter document and port delays can reduce vessel waiting, demurrage and working-capital drag, provided the new operating model delivers measurable turn-time gains. For new entrants, a buyer-backed farm-services and traceability model in the Eastern and Central cocoa belt is more defensible than speculative bean buying; validate farmer participation, quality premiums, offtake and payment terms before scaling.

## 3. Nigeria’s campus microgrid offers a bankable template; Ghana’s mega-project is still a proposal

Nigeria commissioned a 3.3 MWp solar hybrid mini-grid with 2 MWh of battery storage at Yakubu Gowon University, including a 3 MW AC output, grid connection and training centre. It is a delivered institutional system, rather than a pipeline announcement. By contrast, Ghana’s proposed 270 MW Gomoa solar project remains at preparation stage: its reported end-2027 first-phase target does not establish financial close, completed permits, grid arrangements or a signed power-purchase agreement. Solad also reported more than 1,800 solar home systems installed in Nigeria in 2026, with further rollout planned.

**Why it matters:** The campus project demonstrates a procurement archetype that can be adapted for hospitals, universities and industrial facilities: combine solar generation and storage for critical loads, then judge the economics on uptime, diesel displacement, collections and lifecycle cost—not nameplate capacity alone. Developers and service firms can pursue smaller, repeatable behind-the-meter contracts while building local O&M and battery-replacement capability. In Ghana, suppliers should treat Gomoa as a prospect, not booked revenue, until offtake, finance, permits and grid connection are evidenced; the reported US$1bn annual cost of delays to energy-sector recovery reinforces counterparty and payment-risk diligence.

## 4. IPO talk is gathering scale, but neither headline offer is ready to price

Market observations available this week were mixed and not fully synchronised: the Ghana Stock Exchange Composite fell 0.50% on 24 September, while the BRVM Composite rose 2.97%; reports also put Nigeria’s NGX benchmark above 250,000 points and market capitalisation at a record ₦162.39 trillion. The most consequential pipeline stories were a reported Dangote Petroleum Refinery offer at ₦525 and Airtel Money’s reported ambition for a London listing at an $8–9 billion valuation. The reviewed evidence did not establish a filed prospectus, confirmed timetable or completed public offer for either.

**Why it matters:** A refinery or mobile-money flotation could widen access to large regional businesses and deepen local capital markets, but an intention or press-reported price is not an allocation opportunity. Investors, advisers and brokers should prepare compliant onboarding and research distribution while waiting for formal offer documents, venue, structure, valuation basis and subscription dates. For portfolio decisions today, headline index gains are not a substitute for issuer-level liquidity, free-float and FX analysis; the GSE and BRVM year-to-date advances sit alongside sharp differences in daily direction and trading depth.

## 5. Import bills underline the opportunity—and the discipline—of local substitution

Latest accessible monthly data put Ghana’s imports at US$1.813 billion in April 2026, Nigeria’s at NGN4.853 trillion in June (down from NGN5.242 trillion in May), and Côte d’Ivoire’s at XOF962.6 billion in May (down from XOF1,123 billion in April). The figures cover different months and currencies, and nominal totals do not reveal physical volumes or commodity-level composition. Ghana’s reported exports reached US$22.41 billion through August, led by gold, cocoa and oil, which may support FX availability but does not remove importers’ exposure to settlement timing, cedi volatility and clearance costs.

**Why it matters:** Food processing and import substitution are credible opportunities only where local supply can meet buyer specifications at a repeatable landed cost. A measured pilot—such as locally milled and packaged rice for institutional buyers—can test savings, quality, working-capital needs and reorder rates before investment in capacity. Importers can also pay for practical support on customs documentation, FX exposure and port-to-warehouse coordination at Tema or Lagos. Do not infer a new tariff or ban from this week’s reporting; validate tariff classification, licensing and shipment economics against current official guidance and supplier quotations.
