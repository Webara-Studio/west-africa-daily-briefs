# West Africa Weekly — 2026 W35

West Africa's commercial story this week was one of selective formalisation: regulators are defining the perimeter for digital assets, governments are courting investment in food and power, and traders are confronting the cost of moving goods through imperfect corridors. The strongest opportunities sit where policy meets execution — compliant settlement, distributed energy, food processing, and more reliable trade infrastructure. Execution quality, rather than headline announcements, will decide who captures value.

## 1. Ghana Moves Crypto Towards Supervised Market Formation

Industry reporting says Ghana's virtual-asset regulatory sandbox has expanded to 20 firms, up from an earlier cohort of 11. The move signals that the Bank of Ghana and SEC Ghana are shifting from regulatory uncertainty towards supervised experimentation, although sandbox participation is not the same as a full licence or permission to offer every crypto service. Nigeria, meanwhile, continues to tighten its perimeter through SEC incubation and approval pathways, with changes to retail P2P activity highlighting the cost of weak compliance and uncertain banking access.

**Why it matters:** The investable opportunity is moving away from another speculative consumer exchange and towards the infrastructure required for legitimate digital finance. Stablecoin settlement, transaction monitoring, travel-rule tooling, safeguarding, local-currency reconciliation and auditable payout through banks or MoMo partners can serve importers, remittance operators, freelancers and diaspora-linked SMEs. Ghana's sandbox creates a useful proving ground, but operators should treat licensing, AML/KYC, custody, cybersecurity and consumer disclosures as core product features — not paperwork to be added later.

## 2. Cocoa Supply Is Heavy; Processing Margins Matter More Than Speculation

ICE cocoa futures fell 4.01% across the latest five-session comparison to about US$5,844 per tonne. At the same time, reported Côte d'Ivoire port arrivals for the 2025–26 season passed 2 million tonnes, around 21% above the comparable point, creating a difficult combination of softer prices and strong near-term supply. Ghana's production outlook remains weaker in market reporting, with a reported possible 16% decline; that figure is a forecast rather than a newly verified COCOBOD estimate.

**Why it matters:** Raw-cocoa exposure is now a poor substitute for a business model. The stronger angle is buyer-led aggregation and primary processing: cleaning, drying, grading, traceability and compliant packaging close to production corridors, backed by contracted offtake. The same logic applies to cashew and shea, where negotiated quality-adjusted prices reward reliable processing rather than headline commodity bets. Investors should secure an anchor buyer, validate rejection and moisture rates, and add working capital only against purchase orders or contracted supply.

## 3. Nigeria's Distributed-Power Market Gains a Bankability Signal

Nigeria is reportedly backing a proposed Renewable Asset Management Company, or RAMCO, intended to attract longer-term private capital into renewable assets. Separately, JICA was reported to have committed US$119 million to expand Nigeria's mini-grid programme. Ghana has also initiated a comprehensive review of its national grid after recent stability concerns, while Nigeria is considering a renewable-energy tariff for universities and teaching hospitals.

**Why it matters:** These developments point towards a market that is becoming less about announcing megawatts and more about ownership, collections and asset performance. The immediate opportunity is not necessarily a merchant solar farm; it is delivery capability around funded programmes and institutional loads. Local EPC and O&M firms, metering providers, battery-replacement specialists, portfolio-monitoring platforms and companies delivering solar-plus-storage to processors, cold stores, hospitals and universities can capture recurring value. The commercial discipline is to win an anchor programme or customer first, then price imported hardware, FX, warranty support and battery replacement honestly.

## 4. Ghana's US$270m Poultry Programme Opens a Value-Chain Test

Ghana has reportedly signed a US$270 million poultry project agreement aimed at reducing chicken imports. The arrangement is described as Heads of Terms, with a proposed 10–12 month development and design period, so it should not yet be treated as financial close, a completed procurement, or guaranteed operating capacity. The project nevertheless places import substitution, local production and food-security infrastructure firmly on the commercial agenda.

**Why it matters:** The opportunity extends well beyond poultry farms. Feed mills, hatcheries, veterinary inputs, farmer onboarding, cold-chain logistics, processing, quality assurance and distribution will determine whether the programme can compete with imported chicken. Suppliers should seek the financing structure, named operating partners, offtake arrangements, land and environmental approvals, procurement timetable and local-participation requirements before committing capital. A smaller, contract-backed service or supply position is more sensible than building speculative capacity ahead of the programme's bankability.

## 5. Preferential Access Can Shift Trade Flows — If Landed Costs Behave

Nigerian media report that China imported approximately US$2.25 billion of Nigerian goods in the first six months after zero-tariff treatment, an 81% year-on-year increase. In Ghana, importers and exporters are seeking an urgent meeting over a disputed GH¢720 container charge, illustrating how a seemingly modest destination cost can damage margins when multiplied across low-margin consignments. Across the region, food, fuel, machinery, medicines and electronics continue to show resilient underlying demand, but FX, port charges, demurrage and non-tariff barriers remain decisive.

**Why it matters:** Tariff preferences are valuable only when a business can qualify, document and deliver consistently. For Ghanaian operators, the more defensible opportunity is a landed-cost and import-substitution platform: consolidate SME cargo through Tema, manage classification and documentation, provide transparent cedi costing, then use repeated shipment data to identify one product suitable for local assembly, refurbishment or processing. Rice milling and packaging inputs, medical consumables, industrial spares and solar-service components are more credible starting points than a broad import ban or speculative bulk inventory. MoMo-enabled collections can improve SME execution, but formal customs and tax compliance remain non-negotiable.
