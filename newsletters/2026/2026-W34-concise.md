# West Africa Weekly Newsletter — Week 34, 2026

West Africa’s commercial story this week was defined by capital moving towards infrastructure, formalisation and regulated access. Nigeria’s potential Dangote Refinery IPO, Ghana’s energy-debt clearance, new off-grid and storage finance, and stronger traceability requirements all point to the same conclusion: the opportunity is real, but execution, documentation and payment security will decide who captures it. For operators, this is a week to favour measured pilots, contracted demand and compliance-ready systems over headline-led expansion.

## 1. Dangote Refinery’s potential US$5bn IPO could reset Nigerian capital markets

Reporting this week points to a targeted October NGX listing for Dangote Refinery, with a potential raise of approximately **US$5 billion** and about **US$1 billion** of backing reported ahead of the offer. The transaction remains a pipeline event: valuation, free float, final timetable, prospectus, allocation rules and any wider African-market participation still require formal confirmation from the issuer, SEC and NGX. The size would make it one of the region’s most consequential private-sector listings, with implications for energy-sector valuation, domestic ownership and the credibility of Nigerian market infrastructure. It could also become a reference point for other African companies considering local listings instead of relying solely on foreign capital.

**Why it matters:** A successful listing would be a major test of Nigeria’s ability to absorb a large primary issue and could deepen domestic, institutional and diaspora participation in local equities. It would also expose the market’s weaknesses — investor education, FX access, settlement capacity and secondary liquidity — creating demand for regulated onboarding, prospectus education, allocation, custody and investor-relations infrastructure. Issuers should remember that an oversubscribed offer is only the beginning; durable value depends on credible disclosure and a functioning market after the opening bell.

## 2. Nigeria’s US$200m off-grid push makes reliable power a services market

Nigeria has received a fresh reported **US$200 million World Bank signal** for off-grid power alongside changes to mini-grid rules, while the programme still faces a substantial connections and execution gap before December 2028. Senegal has also signed deals for two utility-scale solar-plus-battery plants, and Ghana’s reported US$500 million-plus Genser financing and electricity-export ambition reinforce the region’s shift towards more investable power infrastructure.

**Why it matters:** The winners will not necessarily be panel importers. They will be operators who can combine finance, installation, monitoring and maintenance into a dependable service for customers whose alternative is expensive diesel. The sharper opportunity is financed, dependable power for factories, cold stores, agro-processors, telecom sites, irrigation, clinics and small manufacturers — supported by load audits, local technicians, monitoring, collections and battery lifecycle management. Developers and lenders must price the less glamorous risks properly: utility arrears, currency mismatch, tariff collection, degradation, replacement reserves and payment security. Nigeria’s Geregu bond-default reporting is a useful warning that generation demand does not automatically create bankable cash flow.

## 3. Ghana’s US$1.47bn energy-debt clearance is a balance-sheet opportunity — not yet a cure

Ghana’s Government says it has cleared **US$1.47 billion** in energy-sector legacy debt, a potentially important development for generators, fuel suppliers, banks and independent power producers. The commercial outcome depends on which creditors were paid, on what timetable, and whether tariff, levy and state-offtaker reforms prevent arrears from rebuilding; the headline should therefore be treated as a balance-sheet signal rather than proof that sector liquidity is permanently repaired.

**Why it matters:** If settlement discipline improves, suppliers may recover working capital, lenders may reassess risk and industrial users could gain more confidence in power availability and contracting. The immediate opportunity is audit-ready energy, payment and compliance support: creditor reconciliation, contract documentation, tariff analysis, refund tracking and cash-flow monitoring. Businesses should still insist on milestone billing, payment-security protections and evidence of recurring collections before taking unhedged exposure to Ghanaian regulated assets.

## 4. Gold’s US$20bn export engine is raising the value of traceability

Ghana’s 2025 gold exports were reported at roughly **US$20 billion**, with 2026 artisanal output expected to remain above the prior record as GoldBod formalisation gathers pace. At the same time, cocoa remains strategically important but volatile: Côte d’Ivoire is shipping strongly while Ghana and the wider sector face price correction, weather risk and increasing EU anti-deforestation due-diligence pressure. The broader export lesson is that physical flows can stay strong while producer margins weaken, particularly when compliance and imported-input costs rise.

**Why it matters:** The commercial premium is shifting from raw volume to auditable origin, reliable quality and documented compliance. Assay laboratories, responsible-sourcing audits, secure logistics, farm and batch geolocation, chain-of-custody records and semi-processing can capture value that commodity speculation cannot. A Ghana-based platform beginning with traceable cocoa, cashew and shea — using modular drying, grading or processing capacity and two anchor buyers — is a more defensible model than building a large factory or betting the business on the next gold or cocoa price move.

## 5. Stablecoins move closer to regulated settlement infrastructure

Ghana’s reported VASP sandbox and Nigeria’s evolving CBN sandbox and SEC incubation activity are creating controlled routes for serious digital-asset operators, while Yellow Card’s reported **US$40 million** strategic funding and Quidax’s reported expansion across more than **21 countries** point towards institutional stablecoin payments rather than retail speculation. Global BTC and ETH prices were firm but not euphoric, and no clear West Africa-specific spot-price impulse emerged; the more consequential regional movement is compliance, liquidity and settlement infrastructure.

**Why it matters:** Remittance firms, exporters, payment companies and freelancers need predictable local-currency payouts, not another token. The strongest product wedge is partner-led software that reconciles stablecoin, bank and MoMo records; screens wallets; manages KYC, limits and manual reviews; and produces audit-ready regulatory reports while regulated partners handle custody and on/off-ramp activity. Sandbox admission is not full authorisation, so the commercial advantage will belong to operators who map licensing, AML, sanctions, FX and payout-liquidity risk before scaling across corridors.
