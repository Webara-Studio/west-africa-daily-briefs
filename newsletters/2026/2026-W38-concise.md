# West Africa Weekly — 14–20 September 2026

This week’s market signal is clear: capital is moving towards regulated rails, dependable infrastructure and assets that can be financed, while raw policy ambition and commodity exposure remain less investable on their own. Ghana offers the week’s most practical SME-finance opening; Nigeria supplies the largest public-markets and distributed-power catalysts. In both cases, execution—licensing, local settlement, service capability and offtake—will determine who captures the value.

## 1. Dangote’s ₦2.15 Trillion Offer Could Redraw Nigeria’s Equity-Market Map

Multiple reports say Dangote Refinery opened a **₦2.15 trillion public offer** on the Nigerian Exchange (NGX) on 14 September, potentially adding roughly US$60 billion of market capitalisation if completed on the reported terms. It arrives after a softer secondary-market week, with press reports putting the prior weekly NGX index fall at 1.60% and associated market-capitalisation loss at about ₦1.96 trillion. The immediate variables are subscription demand, allocation quality, free float, settlement reliability and an official listing timetable—not the headline valuation alone.

**Why it matters:** This is the region’s clearest near-term test of whether a large African issuer can broaden public-market liquidity and bring retail and diaspora capital into a properly structured offer. The commercial opening sits with licensed brokers, custodians and distribution partners that can provide clear onboarding, KYC/AML, transparent FX, investor education and reporting. A glossy investment app without regulated execution and custody will not retain assets; a compliant access layer could build a valuable repeat-investor base.

## 2. Ghana’s Green Innovation Fund Turns Climate Talk into an SME Finance Route

The EU, Netherlands and Fidelity Bank Ghana have launched a **€6.668 million Green Innovation Fund** to support enterprises adopting green technologies, growing sustainably and competing in domestic, European and AfCFTA markets. It forms part of a €17.3 million Green, Digital and Inclusive Private Sector Development programme launched in January. Separately, President Mahama said the 2027 budget will prioritise nine sectors and envisages about US$2 billion annually for job-creating, largely private-sector-led activity, although neither the legal vehicle nor access rules are yet settled.

**Why it matters:** The fund is a live route for viable Ghanaian SMEs, especially where energy efficiency, cold chain, cleaner production, digital capability and export readiness meet a named buyer or measurable cost saving. The sharper commercial move is project preparation: turn real operating data, site tenure, supplier quotations, offtake evidence and repayment capacity into financeable capex proposals. Do not build speculative factory capacity around budget rhetoric; obtain Fidelity’s eligibility, security, pricing and disbursement terms before committing expenditure.

## 3. Nigeria’s Mini-Grid Capital Signals a Productive-Power Market, Not a Panel Boom

Reporting on 16 September says All On invested **US$5 million** in PowerGen to support Nigerian renewable-energy access and mini-grid expansion. Nigerian outlets also reported the commissioning of a **3 MW hybrid solar mini-grid** at Yakubu Gowon University, an institutional load with potentially stronger daytime utilisation than a household-only system. The retrieved evidence does not yet verify PowerGen’s deployment sites or timetable, nor the university project’s EPC, tariff, battery configuration or operating contract.

**Why it matters:** The investable demand is dependable productive power: solar, properly sized storage, efficient equipment, monitoring, maintenance and repayment collection bundled around a customer whose electricity reliability converts into cashflow. In Ghana, target cold stores, pharmacies, clinics, schools, water suppliers and light manufacturers across the Accra–Tema and Kumasi corridors, with cedi-denominated contracts and explicit FX and battery-replacement provisions. The first gate is three months of measured uptime, collection and avoided generator spend—not a large installed-kilowatt headline.

## 4. Ghana’s Virtual-Asset Sandbox Makes Compliance and Payouts the Real Digital-Asset Play

Ghana’s SEC has published **20 Virtual Asset Sandbox participants** under the Virtual Asset Service Providers Act, 2025, covering pilots from tokenisation and brokerage to exchange and trading-platform services. The Bank of Ghana’s Virtual Assets Coordinating Committee is intended to add the central-bank and inter-agency operating layer, with full operationalisation directed towards 2027. This is supervised market entry, not blanket operating permission: the SEC is still using sandbox data to shape activity-specific licensing and registration rules.

**Why it matters:** The sensible opportunity is B2B infrastructure for controlled participants and payment operators—KYC case management, sanctions screening, transaction monitoring, cedi reconciliation, auditable reporting and reliable bank or MoMo payouts. Stablecoin remittance and tokenisation activity may create real settlement demand, but the commercial winner will be the operator that can document a compliant transfer from payer to final recipient. Founders should avoid consumer-exchange or anonymous-P2P narratives and instead prove one tightly defined, authorised corridor with funds held by a permitted partner.

## 5. Export Margin Is Shifting from Raw Volume to Proven, Buyer-Ready Supply

West Africa’s hard-currency export base remains concentrated in gold, cocoa, bauxite and Nigerian crude, but the more durable SME opportunity is semi-processed, traceable product. Cocoa remains supply-constrained and cashew, shea, rubber and timber buyers are increasingly demanding clear origin, quality and compliance evidence. Tema and Abidjan retain strong gateway roles, yet for smaller exporters the decisive frictions are inland haulage, document accuracy, testing, inspection, terminal timing, working capital and container availability—not simply port capacity.

**Why it matters:** The defensible margin is between informal, inconsistent supply and a lot that a named buyer can put straight into a production line or premium retail channel with confidence. Build around one specification—such as food-grade refined shea butter, W320/W240 cashew kernels or cocoa nibs—with lot records, third-party testing, clear Incoterms and a reliable forwarder. Sell samples before accumulating stock, then add consolidation and documentation services once repeat orders prove the economics; a cheap FOB quote that omits delay, rework and finance costs is not a commercial offer.
