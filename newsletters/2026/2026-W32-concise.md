# West Africa Weekly — 2026 W32

West Africa’s commercial story this week was a shift from broad ambition to execution: capital markets are preparing for larger issuers, energy policy is producing more bankable demand, and digital-asset regulation is creating a route for compliant infrastructure. At the same time, regional fragmentation and commodity concentration are raising the premium on local compliance, reliable logistics and verifiable data.

## 1. Dangote Refinery Targets a Potential $5bn October IPO

Nigeria’s Dangote Refinery is reportedly targeting an October 2026 listing that could raise approximately **$5bn**, potentially making it one of Africa’s largest equity offerings in recent years. The proposed pan-African strategy, exchange choice, valuation, free float, advisers and final prospectus remain unconfirmed, so this is a reported target rather than a completed transaction. The week also brought a strong completed-market signal: Bridge Bank’s roughly **$118m** BRVM share sale reportedly sold out in under 24 hours.

**Why it matters**

A Dangote Refinery flotation would be more than a Nigerian IPO. It could deepen domestic ownership, test the ability of African exchanges to absorb strategic industrial assets and create a substantial new pipeline for pension funds, institutional investors and diaspora capital. It would also sharpen demand for cross-border custody, FX-transparent brokerage, investor-relations support and listing-readiness services. Businesses should prepare for the opportunity without pricing in an offer that has not yet been formally approved.

## 2. Ghana’s 1.5GW Industrial Solar Push Makes Power a Service Market

Ghana has reportedly begun construction on the first **100MW** of a proposed **1.5GW** solar programme for industry, with associated reporting pointing to guaranteed solar offtake for manufacturers. The country is also reported to be planning procurement of **200MW of battery storage**. Elsewhere, Senegal’s 60MW Kolda solar-plus-storage project and Côte d’Ivoire’s project-finance green-bond precedent show how structured capital is beginning to support larger renewable assets.

**Why it matters**

The commercially valuable shift is from selling panels to selling dependable industrial uptime. Manufacturers, cold stores, agro-processors, telecoms sites and hotels can pay for reduced diesel use, better power quality and predictable availability, provided payment security and grid interfaces are properly designed. The immediate opportunity is a Ghana pilot combining solar, batteries, remote monitoring, maintenance and local-currency billing, then using measured uptime and savings data to raise development-finance or bank capital. The risks remain serious: procurement terms, ECG and grid coordination, imported-equipment FX exposure and the final treatment of offtake guarantees will determine bankability.

## 3. Ghana’s VASP Sandbox Opens a New Market for Compliance Infrastructure

Ghana’s first virtual-asset service-provider sandbox has reportedly admitted **11 firms**, while Nigeria is moving towards more coordinated virtual-assets oversight through a presidential executive order and additional Securities and Exchange Commission incubation approvals. Stablecoin payment infrastructure is also gaining commercial attention, with reporting that Quidax is extending rails to 21 countries and that Mastercard and Yellow Card are targeting African payment and remittance flows. Global spot markets were comparatively quiet: on 4 August BTC was approximately **$63,374** and ETH **$1,856**, with USDT and USDC close to their dollar pegs.

**Why it matters**

The investable opportunity is not another lightly differentiated retail exchange. Sandbox participants and regulated remittance firms will need customer due diligence, wallet screening, transaction monitoring, reconciliation, suspicious-transaction workflows, consumer disclosures and audit-ready reporting. A Ghana- or Nigeria-first platform that connects licensed on/off-ramps with bank and MoMo records could earn B2B platform and transaction fees while avoiding informal custody. The key discipline is regulatory classification: sandbox admission is not full authorisation, and stablecoin settlement only works where local liquidity, compliant cash-out and sanctions controls are dependable.

## 4. Nigeria’s Digital-Sovereignty Rules and Ghana’s NPL Deadline Create a Trust-Infrastructure Cycle

Nigeria is reportedly pressing banks and payment firms to keep payment data and a substantial share of cloud spending onshore, alongside a reported requirement for Nigerian telcos to maintain dedicated cybersecurity budgets. In Ghana, the Bank of Ghana is reported to have set **December 2026** as the deadline for banks to reduce non-performing loans to **10%**. Both developments remain implementation-sensitive until binding circulars, technical standards and enforcement details are published.

**Why it matters**

For Nigeria’s fintech, banking and software ecosystem, localisation is not simply a hosting decision: it affects disaster recovery, cyber resilience, audit trails, vendor selection and operating cost. That creates demand for migration, compliance reporting, security monitoring and regulator-ready documentation, particularly from providers able to operate country-specific controls rather than sell a generic regional architecture. Ghana’s NPL deadline should accelerate loan recoveries, portfolio sales, restructuring tools and alternative-credit underwriting, while making banks more selective about receivables and counterparty quality. The broader lesson is that regional expansion now requires a compliance module, data plan and banking relationship for each market.

## 5. Exporters Can Capture More Value by Selling Traceability, Not Just Commodities

West African export earnings remain concentrated in gold, cocoa, crude oil and bauxite, although Ghana’s central bank reportedly put foreign-exchange reserves at approximately **$12.9bn** and described a stronger first-half trade surplus. The sharper market trend is the premium on compliant, traceable and partially processed shipments: cocoa, rubber and timber exporters face rising deforestation-data requirements, while cashew, shea and food-ingredient buyers are demanding consistent grades, moisture control and documentation.

**Why it matters**

The strongest near-term trade model is an asset-light export-readiness and aggregation platform, beginning with one commodity, one buyer specification and one reliable corridor to Tema or another compliant port. Village collection can combine calibrated weighing, moisture testing, batch IDs, farm or origin data, transparent MoMo payments and laboratory-tested buyer dossiers. Revenue can come from aggregation margins, documentation, managed logistics and quality premiums rather than speculation on commodity prices. This is also a practical AfCFTA play: the value lies in reducing rejected lots, customs friction, payment uncertainty and port delays, not in repeating the free-trade narrative.
